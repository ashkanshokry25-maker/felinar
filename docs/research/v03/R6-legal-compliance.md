# R6 - Legal and compliance for self-owned identity and encrypted per-visitor records

- **Track:** v0.3 research, topic legal-compliance (R6). **Date:** 2026-10-20. **Applies to:** magnet-web 0.2.1 plus the planned v0.3 features: self-owned P-256 identity, site-ledger login, per-visitor encrypted auth and profile records, live peer chat, and files in the site's swarm.
- **This is research, not legal advice.** Nobody qualified in any jurisdiction has reviewed it. The owner is in Canada. Several 2026 items below rest on one secondary source and are marked "unverified". Every item that decides a design choice should go to counsel before the first public release. Status of 2026-10-24: the owner decided not to engage a lawyer for 0.3.0, so that did not happen; a research review by AI agents (2026-10-22 and 2026-10-24) used this note as one of its sources.
- **Method:** WebSearch only this session (search snippets and secondary commentary; the primary PDFs and statute pages were not opened). Where a statute or judgment is cited from background knowledge and not re-read this session, it is marked "background, not re-checked". Access date for every URL: 2026-10-20.
- **Confidence scale:** High = statute or regulator text known and consistent across sources; Medium = two or more secondary sources agree; Low = one secondary source, or background knowledge only.
- **Builds on, does not repeat:** docs/PRIVACY.md (recipients register, controller reading of Fashion ID, DPIA and Quebec notes), docs/research/PRODUCTION-DIGEST.md sections 4.2, 5.3, 9.2, docs/research/MIGRATION-DIGEST.md (L3, L7, Q31, M41), docs/ARCHITECTURE.md section 6.1 (tombstone and salted `vh`/`z`), docs/research/w2c-03-identity-ux.md.

## Summary

1. **Erasure on an append-only log is answerable, but only by design, not by promise.** The EDPB's guidelines on processing through distributed-ledger technologies (version 2.0 reported adopted 2026-07-07; the April 2025 draft changed little per one firm) say the technology does not excuse non-compliance and point to off-chain storage and key destruction where deletion is impossible. magnet-web's tombstone (drop `v` and `z`, keep `{e, h, s}`) is the right shape, but the kept stub still contains the author's public key and the entry metadata. Treat that as personal data until counsel says otherwise.
2. **Encrypted personal data is still personal data in the hands of whoever can decrypt it, and may remain so for everyone while the key exists.** The CJEU's EDPS v SRB judgment (C-413/23 P, 2025-09-04) keeps an identifiability test that depends on the recipient, which helps a visitor who holds only ciphertext they cannot open. It does not help the site owner or the user who holds the key. So per-visitor encrypted records must be wrapped under a key the visitor controls, and key deletion must be a first-class, documented erasure path (crypto-shredding), with the honest statement that copies of ciphertext persist.
3. **Roles are the weakest legal fit, and the docs should say so.** Every visitor holding a replica makes "controller or processor" unclear. The defensible reading: the site owner is the controller (or joint controller with the hub) for the processing the loader causes and for the ledger's purpose; a visitor relaying other people's entries is a recipient or, for purely personal activity, within the household exemption, but a visitor who runs a pin node, a relay or a hub is not. Russmedia (C-492/23, 2025-12-02) shows the CJEU will treat a platform operator as controller of user-posted personal data and will not let the hosting safe harbour excuse GDPR duties. Plan for the owner being treated as controller.
4. **Data minimisation is the strongest lever, and v0.3 is where it is tested.** Keep personal data out of public ledger values (already the rule), store the auth and profile record as ciphertext addressed to the visitor's own key, avoid stable cross-site identifiers (per-site derived keys), and make every optional field optional. A login record that contains only a public key and a nonce is far easier to defend than one with a name and an email.
5. **DPIA is likely mandatory for any site that turns on chat, files or profiles.** The WP248 criteria (innovative technology, large scale, systematic monitoring, vulnerable people) are plausibly met. The Quebec private-sector Act requires a privacy impact assessment for a new information system (s. 3.3). Ship a DPIA template and a pre-filled risk list with v0.3.
6. **Canada: PIPEDA is still the federal law; Bill C-36 (tabled 2026-06-15) would replace it and is not law.** Quebec Law 25 already applies to a Quebec audience and has the strictest defaults. Status of C-36 after 2026-09-21 was not confirmed. Do not write C-36 duties into the docs as current law.
7. **Site owners and hub operators are in scope of the EU DSA and UK Online Safety Act** as hosting or user-to-user services where visitors post. Micro and small enterprise relief exists for the DSA online-platform section (Art. 19) but not for hosting duties. "No backend" does not remove the owner, who still publishes the loader and holds the moderation power.
8. **Age assurance: a system without accounts cannot verify age, and must not pretend to.** The honest options are (a) do not offer chat, file upload or profile features on sites aimed at or likely to be used by children, (b) let the site owner plug in an external age-assurance provider or an EU age-verification app proof as a gate before the SDK features unlock, and (c) default the example site to host-only for children. Ofcom and the ICO (joint statement 2026-03-25) say self-declaration alone is not enough where age matters.
9. **Encryption law is moving in both directions in 2026.** EU: the interim "chat control 1.0" regime lapsed on 2026-04-03 and a replacement Regulation 2026/1881 is reported adopted on 2026-07-23 with an express carve-out for end-to-end encrypted interpersonal communications (single source, unverified); the permanent CSA Regulation is still in trilogue, and the Commission's encryption technology roadmap is promised for 2026. UK: Ofcom published section 121 technology-notice guidance on 2026-05-08; whether any notice has been issued is unverified. The design should state that it cannot scan end-to-end encrypted chat and has no key escrow, and name that as a legal risk for hub operators.
10. **Export controls are light for this project, with one open question.** Publicly available encryption source code is not subject to the EAR (15 CFR 742.15(b)(1)). The e-mail notice is required only for source code that provides or performs "non-standard cryptography" (742.15(b)(2), eCFR read on 2026-10-22; the limit comes from the rule of 2021-03-29, 86 FR 16482). The condition this note set, to re-check if a custom construction is added, has been met: v0.3 added protocols of its own over standard primitives (`src/chat/noise.js`, `src/chat/seal.js`). Whether they are "non-standard cryptography" is not settled by the rule text, so the notice is recommended. See SECURITY.md, "Cryptography and export rules".

## Findings

### F1. Erasure against a hash-linked, replicated log (GDPR Art. 17, UK GDPR Art. 17)

- **Source of the duty.** Art. 17 GDPR (Regulation 2016/679) gives the right to erasure on listed grounds (consent withdrawn, no longer necessary, objection, unlawful processing). Art. 17(2) requires a controller who made data public to take "reasonable steps", considering available technology and cost, to tell other controllers to erase. The "reasonable steps" wording is the doctrinal hook for a design where erasure is best effort across peers. Background, not re-checked this session. Confidence: High on text, Medium on application.
- **Regulator position on shared ledgers.** EDPB Guidelines 02/2025 on processing of personal data through blockchain technologies: final version 2.0 reported adopted 2026-07-07, with the April 2025 draft's substance largely kept. Reported points: the technology does not excuse non-compliance; participants must be able to honour access, rectification and erasure; where deletion is infeasible, make the data effectively unidentifiable (erase the off-chain component, destroy keys); if rights cannot be ensured, consider another architecture; rectification may require erasure. Sources: https://ppc.land/edpb-forces-blockchain-firms-to-avoid-storing-personal-data-on-chain/ and https://thelens.slaughterandmay.com/post/102ndb2/edpb-finalises-guidelines-on-gdpr-and-blockchain and https://www.mondaq.com/fin-tech/1617044/blockchain-and-gdpr-edpb-guidelines-022025-adopted (all accessed 2026-10-20). The search tool could not open the EDPB PDF (reported path: edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-07/edpb_guidelines_202502_blockchain_v2_en.pdf); read it before quoting. Confidence: Medium. Note that the Guidelines are about "blockchain technologies" in their title; this project calls its structure a site ledger and the docs should keep doing so, citing the Guidelines by their official title only.
- **UK.** The ICO ran a consultation on distributed-ledger guidance from 2025-08-28 to 2025-11-07; its draft says wallet, contract and transaction identifiers may be personal data. The ICO page now presents the guidance as final, but the final date and the erasure wording were not confirmed. Source: https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/uk-ico-launches-consultation-on-distributed-ledger-technologies-guidance and https://cy.ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-sharing/distributed-ledger-technologies/about-this-guidance/ (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Low to Medium.
- **Crypto-shredding.** If the payload is stored only as ciphertext under a key that exists only on the data subject's device (and optionally an owner-held recovery wrap), deleting the key makes the ciphertext unreadable to everyone. Regulators accept this as a route to "effective" erasure only when the remaining ciphertext cannot be re-identified or decrypted by anyone (EDPB 02/2025 as reported above). Caveats that the docs must state: (a) key deletion is only as complete as the key's copies (exports, backups, other devices); (b) ciphertext size, timing and links remain as metadata; (c) future cryptanalysis is out of scope for a promise made today; (d) if the visitor exported the identity (Settings export, passphrase-protected), they hold copies they may forget about. Confidence: Medium (reasoning plus regulator direction; no case law found testing it for a peer-to-peer log).
- **The kept stub.** The stub's `e.author` is a stable public key. Under Breyer (C-582/14, 2016-10-19, background, not re-checked) and the SRB reasoning below, a pseudonymous key is personal data for a party who can link it to a person. For the owner, who may also run a login flow that links keys to accounts, the stub is likely personal data. Two design mitigations: per-site derived author keys so the stub cannot be joined across sites, and a "rotate and orphan" action that retires the key (new key signs a retirement note; old key's stubs remain but are no longer linked to a live identity). Neither makes the stub anonymous. Say so.

### F2. Pseudonymised and encrypted data: whose view counts

- **EDPS v SRB (C-413/23 P, 2025-09-04).** Court set aside the General Court's annulment and referred back. Commentary agrees the Court kept a recipient-specific analysis: pseudonymised data need not be personal data "in all cases and for every person", yet remains personal data for a controller who holds the means to re-identify; transparency duties about recipients are assessed from the controller's viewpoint at collection. Sources: https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/talking-tech/en/articles/2025/09/pseudonymized-data-after-edps-v-srb.html and https://www.jonesday.com/de/insights/2025/09/cjeu-clarifies-scope-of-personal-data-in-edps-v-srb-decision and https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/eu-the-srb-decision-a-new-era-for-personal-data-and-data-processing-agreements (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Medium (judgment text not opened).
- **What this means here.** A visitor's replica holds other people's ciphertext they cannot open: arguably not personal data to that visitor. The owner and the data subject can open (or can obtain the key for) the same record: personal data to them. This supports "every visitor is a mere recipient of ciphertext" as a defensible line, but it does not move the owner's own duties. The EDPB ledger guidance (MIGRATION-DIGEST L3) still treats on-ledger encrypted personal data as in scope; the two positions are compatible if the owner is the controller and visitors are recipients.
### F3. Controller, joint controller, processor, recipient when every visitor holds a copy

- **Ground rules.** Art. 4(7) and 4(8) define controller and processor; Art. 26 joint controllers; CJEU Wirtschaftsakademie (C-210/16), Jehovah's Witnesses (C-25/17) and Fashion ID (C-40/17, 2019-07-29) read "determines purposes and means" broadly, including for a site embedding a third-party tool. Fashion ID is already cited in PRIVACY.md. All background, not re-checked. Confidence: High on text.
- **Russmedia (C-492/23, 2025-12-02).** Grand Chamber: the operator of an online marketplace is a controller for personal data in user-posted adverts; the e-Commerce hosting safe harbour cannot be used to avoid GDPR responsibility; pre-publication checks for Art. 9 data were discussed; reasoning leaned on the operator's own terms allowing it to use the content. Sources: https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/cjeus-russmedia-decision-how-online-marketplace-platforms-could-be-held-liable-under-the-gdpr and https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2025/12/Are-Operators-of-Online-Marketplaces-Responsible-for-User-Content-Under-the-GDPR and https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/2/cjeus-russmedia-decision-expands-platform-controller-duties-under-gdpr (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Medium. Implication: a site owner who runs an open ledger with chat and profiles should assume controller status for visitor-posted personal data, even though no server holds it. Commentators doubt the proportionality for small operators; do not rely on that doubt.
- **Visitors as relays.** A visitor who is a natural person acting in a purely personal capacity may fall within the household exemption (Art. 2(2)(c); Lindqvist C-101/01 and Rynes C-212/13 narrow it where data is made available to an indefinite number of people; background, not re-checked). Seeding a public ledger to strangers is closer to Lindqvist than to a private address book. Practical position: the docs must not tell visitors they are "just relaying". They should say that seeding means sharing what the site's ledger contains, and keep the consent card in front of it (already the design in PRIVACY.md).
- **Hubs.** A hub operator who hosts many sites and runs trackers, pin nodes or relays is plausibly a processor for site owners for some functions and a controller for its own logs (PRIVACY.md "Who is responsible for what"). A hub needs a data processing agreement (Art. 28) template; none ships yet (gap).
- **Owner-held recovery or escrow wraps.** If the owner holds any key that can decrypt visitors' records (a recovery wrap, a moderator view), the owner is the controller of the decrypted data and has a security-of-processing duty (Art. 32). The design should avoid owner-held wraps by default. If offered, they are opt-in and described plainly.
- **Peers see IP addresses.** Breyer (C-582/14) treats dynamic IPs as personal data when lawful means to identify exist; Fashion ID attributes the disclosure-by-embedding to the embedder. Both already cited in PRIVACY.md; chat and file swarms add more peers who learn IPs and per-conversation activity. Add this to the recipients register: each chat peer, each file-swarm peer, each relay.

### F4. Lawful basis, consent, ePrivacy

- Storing keys and ledger copies in IndexedDB is an ePrivacy Art. 5(3) question (EDPB Guidelines 2/2023; PRIVACY.md; MIGRATION-DIGEST Q31 stays open).
- For chat and files, the clean basis per feature is consent given at the point of use (opt in to chat; opt in to upload), with a withdrawal control that actually does something (leave presence, stop seeding, delete key). Consent is not a basis for the site's essential delivery; the web seed covers that, which is why legitimate interest is weak for peer upload (PRIVACY.md).
- Minimisation (Art. 5(1)(c), Art. 25): see Summary 4; document defaults and reasons; public-key and timestamp metadata are unavoidable in a signed log, so say so.
- Special categories (Art. 9): a free-form profile or chat can carry them; no free-text profile fields by default.

### F6. DPIA and similar assessments

- GDPR Art. 35: a DPIA is required where processing is likely to result in high risk; the WP248 rev.01 criteria (endorsed by the EDPB) suggest one when two criteria are met. For chat, files and profiles on an open ledger: innovative technology, large scale (depends on site), systematic monitoring (swarm membership visible), vulnerable data subjects (children). Background, not re-checked. Confidence: Medium.
- Quebec: s. 3.3 of the private-sector Act requires a privacy impact assessment for a project of acquisition, development or redesign of an information system or electronic service delivery involving personal information; s. 17 adds an assessment before communicating personal information outside Quebec. The CAI published a PIA guide on 2023-09-22 (French only), not mandatory in form. Sources: https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/quebec-privacy-regulators-guide-law-25-compliant-privacy-impact-assessments (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Medium. A peer swarm inherently communicates IP addresses outside Quebec, so a Quebec-facing site should do both assessments.
### F7. Canada: PIPEDA, Quebec Law 25, and pending reform

- **PIPEDA** (S.C. 2000, c. 5) still governs commercial activity in provinces without a substantially similar law. Principles: consent, limiting collection, safeguards, access and correction, accountability, openness. There is no stand-alone erasure right equal to Art. 17; the Privacy Commissioner has taken the view that deletion and de-indexing rights flow from the accuracy and retention principles (background, not re-checked). Confidence: Low to Medium.
- **Bill C-36** (Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act), reported tabled 2026-06-15: would replace PIPEDA, create a Commission with order-making power, penalties up to C$10 million or 3% of global revenue, a private right of action; consent stays the default. Reported status: needs committee, Senate and royal assent; Parliament reconvened 2026-09-21; stage on 2026-10-20 not confirmed. Sources: https://www.cippic.ca/news/bill-c-36%3A-the-government-proposes-a-new-private-sector-privacy-law and https://www.bennettjones.com/Insights/Blogs/Bill-C-36-and-Canadian-Privacy-Reform and https://ppc.land/canadas-bill-c-36-sets-10-million-privacy-fines-as-parliament-returns/ (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Low to Medium. Earlier attempts (C-11, C-27) died on the order paper.
- **Quebec Law 25** (Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector, as amended by Law 25): privacy impact assessments (s. 3.3), a person in charge of personal information protection (default: the highest-ranking person), incident register and notice for confidentiality incidents with risk of serious injury, technology that identifies, locates or profiles must be disclosed with the means to activate it (s. 8.1), privacy-protective defaults for services offered to the public with parameters (s. 9.1), data portability (in force 2024-09), right to cessation of dissemination and de-indexation (s. 28.1, background; this session's search could not verify it), and automated-decision notice. Monetary penalties up to C$25 million or 4% of worldwide turnover and a private right of action with punitive damages (background, not re-checked). PRIVACY.md already covers 3.3, 8.1 and 9.1. Confidence: Medium on the list, Low on s. 28.1 without a primary check.
- **Application to a static site with a swarm.** Quebec duty depends on enterprise carrying on activity in Quebec and personal information about Quebec residents; the owner being in Canada makes the Act's reach realistic. The "most private by default" rule (s. 9.1) is met by the blocking gate when `--aud qc` is set (PRIVACY.md). For chat and files, defaults must be off until the visitor opts in.
- **Breach notification.** PIPEDA s. 10.1 requires reporting breaches that create a real risk of significant harm; Quebec requires notice to the CAI and individuals for a risk of serious injury. A key leak or a malicious peer decrypting group chat is a breach in either regime only if the owner holds the data or the key; otherwise the owner has little to report. This is a reason to avoid owner-held wraps (F3).

### F8. United States state laws and sector rules

- **Comprehensive privacy statutes.** Secondary sources put the count at about 20 in effect and 23 to 24 enacted by mid-2026 (Indiana, Kentucky and Rhode Island took effect 2026-01-01; a 2026-06 article lists Alabama, Louisiana, Oklahoma and Vermont as new). Sources: https://www.bytebacklaw.com/2026/06/u-s-state-privacy-law-landscape-expands-to-24-states-what-the-latest-legislative-wave-means-for-businesses/ and https://www.enzuzo.com/blog/us-state-privacy-laws (accessed 2026-10-20). The counts disagree; use "about 20 in effect, more enacted" and link the IAPP tracker, not a number. Confidence: Low to Medium.
- **Thresholds.** Most state laws apply above a consumer-count or revenue threshold, so a small owner may fall outside many of them; the laws that apply to any size (a few) and the children's provisions are the ones to check. A visitor's IP address and device identifier count as personal data in most of these laws. Rights typically include access, deletion, correction and opt-out of sale or targeted advertising; sensitive-data consent applies to precise geolocation and children's data. Background, not re-checked.

### F9. EU DSA and the UK Online Safety Act for owners and hub operators

- **DSA (Regulation 2022/2065).** Arts. 4 to 6 give conditional liability exemptions for mere conduit, caching and hosting. A site that stores visitors' posts and gives the owner moderation power is a hosting service; Art. 16 notice-and-action and Art. 17 statement of reasons apply to hosting providers; online platforms (hosting plus public dissemination) get Arts. 19 to 28 on top, unless a micro or small enterprise (Recommendation 2003/361/EC), subject to Art. 19 and Art. 24(3); 12 months of grace after losing status. Source: https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/dsa/dsa-regulation/article-19/ and https://presencis.com/regulations/dsa/article-19/ (accessed 2026-10-20); one compliance page gave an inconsistent scope for Art. 19 and was discarded. Confidence: Medium. Peer-served ledger entries blur "stored at the request of a recipient" but the owner's moderators can order removal, which is the control the DSA looks for. Russmedia shows the hosting exemption does not cover GDPR duties.
- **UK Online Safety Act 2023.** Applies to user-to-user services with links to the UK, regardless of where the provider is. Duties include an illegal-content risk assessment, proportionate safety measures, a reporting and complaints route, and, for services likely to be accessed by children, a children's access assessment and children's risk assessment. Ofcom's section 121 guidance on technology notices was published 2026-05-08 with the 2026-27 priorities (children, terrorism and hate, women and girls). Sources: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/roadmap-to-regulation/ and https://www.mondaq.com/uk/social-media/1794366/ofcom-publishes-article-detailing-its-2026-priorities-for-online-safety-update-on-online-safety-act-implementation-plans-and-guidance-regarding-technology-notices (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Medium. Small services are not exempt from the illegal-content duties; the proportionality principle scales the measures. Whether a decentralised host-less site counts as having a "provider" is unsettled; the owner who publishes the loader and the moderation key is the obvious candidate. Treat that as "probably yes".
- **Practical consequence.** File upload and chat bring the strongest exposure: child sexual abuse material and terrorism content are the categories Ofcom names for scanning-based measures, and an end-to-end encrypted design with no backend cannot offer scanning. The honest document says the owner can moderate public ledger entries and public file listings, cannot read private chat, and must therefore keep private-sharing features small, opt-in and limited to authenticated visitors.

### F10. Age verification and digital identity (2025-2026)

- **EU.** DSA Art. 28 guidelines (July 2025) are the Commission's benchmark. On 2026-04-29 a non-binding policy document encouraged member states to give EU users privacy-preserving age verification by 2026-12-31 based on anonymous proof of age; the "mini-wallet" age-verification blueprint was technically ready 2026-04-15, with pilots in Denmark, France, Greece, Italy and Spain. Source: https://fpf.org/blog/the-eu-commissions-approach-to-age-verification-mobile-apps-dsa-enforcement-and-challenging-national-social-media-bans/ (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Medium. The European Digital Identity Wallet deadline was not confirmed this session; check Regulation (EU) 2024/1183 and the member-state schedule.
- **UK.** Ofcom and the ICO issued a joint statement on age assurance on 2026-03-25: self-declaration alone is not enough where children's access matters; a service that does not enforce a minimum age with highly effective age assurance must assume children are present and reflect it in its risk assessment. Ofcom's report of 2026-07-16 covers the first six months of the children's duties. Sources: https://www.insideglobaltech.com/2026/03/27/ofcom-and-ico-issue-joint-statement-on-age-assurance/ and https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/data-protection/summer-2026/ofcom-and-ico-joint-statement-on-age-assurance/ and https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ofcom-report-on-the-use-of-age-assurance (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Medium.
- **US.** App-store accountability laws (Utah, Texas, Louisiana; California later): Texas's was preliminarily enjoined; Utah's applies from 2026 with enforcement from 2026-12-31; developers receive an age signal and are then treated as having actual knowledge of the age range. Source: https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2025/07/Texas-and-Louisiana-Join-Growing-Trend-of-State-Age-Verification-Laws-for-App-Stores and https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/app-store-accountability-acts/ (accessed 2026-10-20). Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton (U.S. Supreme Court, June 2025) upheld Texas's age verification for sites with sexual content under intermediate scrutiny (background, not re-checked this session). A browser-delivered site is not in an app store, so the app-store laws bite only if the owner also ships an installable wrapper.
- **How a system with no accounts meets or avoids these laws.** It does not meet them by itself. A static loader cannot check age. What it can do: (1) offer an SDK hook that lets the owner require a verifiable credential from an external provider or a wallet proof before unlocking chat, files or profiles, and record only a boolean in the ledger; (2) default every community feature off on any site tagged `--aud kids` or `--aud general` without an age gate; (3) refuse to ship a self-declaration gate as if it were assurance. The identity in v0.3 is a key, not a person, so the system must never claim "verified adult".
### F11. Encryption and lawful access debates (2026)

- **EU chat control.** The interim derogation (Regulation 2021/1232 extended) lapsed on 2026-04-03 after Parliament rejected an extension on 2026-03-26 (228 for, 311 against, 92 abstentions). eucrim reports that on 2026-07-23 the Council accepted Parliament's amendments to a new Regulation 2026/1881 that expressly does not apply to interpersonal communications to which end-to-end encryption is, has been, or will be applied. The permanent CSA Regulation is still in trilogue (started 2025-12-09); the Council's 2025 position moved to a risk-based approach and away from broad detection orders. Sources: https://eucrim.eu/news/interim-rules-on-voluntary-csam-detection-reinstated-until-2028/ and https://eucrim.eu/news/csa-regulation-council-position-reached/ and https://euperspectives.eu/2026/03/meps-extend-chat-control-limit-scanning/ and https://www.cife.eu/Ressources/FCK/files/publications/policy%20paper/2026/191_Stehlikova_The_Termination_of_Chat_Control_1_0_CIFE_2026.pdf (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Low on the July reinstatement (single source, flagged by the search tool as needing Official Journal verification); Medium on the lapse and trilogue.
- **EU lawful access.** ProtectEU (2025-04) committed to a Technology Roadmap on encryption in 2026; the 2025-06-24 roadmap on lawful access plans a Europol decryption capability from 2030; the expert group reports in 2026. No published roadmap found. Sources: https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-presents-roadmap-effective-and-lawful-access-data-law-enforcement-2025-06-24_en and https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies/internal-security/lawful-access-data/encryption_en (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Medium.
- **UK.** OSA s. 121 allows Ofcom to require accredited technology to identify terrorism or CSEA content on user-to-user services, with accuracy minimums; the guidance came out 2026-05-08; the claim that a first notice has been issued is unverified (an opinion piece only), and a secondary history says Ofcom signalled no notices until suitable technology exists. Apple's UK iCloud encryption withdrawal came under the Investigatory Powers Act, not the OSA. Sources: Ofcom roadmap page and Mondaq summary above, https://www.techradar.com/vpn/vpn-privacy-security/ofcom-wants-to-double-down-on-file-monitoring-in-2026 (accessed 2026-10-20). Confidence: Medium on the power and guidance, Low on use.
- **Implication.** A design with end-to-end encrypted chat between identities and no server in the middle means (a) no content scanning is possible by the owner, (b) no key escrow exists, (c) a notice to scan or decrypt cannot be satisfied technically. The exposure is therefore regulatory, borne by whoever is treated as the provider. The docs must say plainly that the project will not add a back door, that the owner can only moderate public entries, and that this can conflict with a notice in some jurisdictions. That is a business-risk statement for owners, not a promise of immunity.

### F12. Export of cryptography from an open-source project

- US EAR: publicly available encryption source code classified under ECCN 5D002 became "not subject to the EAR" under the 2016 restructuring that created 15 CFR 742.15(b) (81 FR 64656, 2016-09-20); the rule of 2021-03-29 (86 FR 16482, which implemented the Wassenaar Arrangement's 2019 plenary decisions) removed the e-mail notice for publicly available encryption source code except for "non-standard cryptography". Sources read on 2026-10-22: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-15/section-742.15, https://www.federalregister.gov/d/2021-05481 and https://media.bis.gov/media/documents/table-changes-enc-wa2019-rule-final-version.pdf. The earlier secondary sources (Jones Day, Thompson Hine, federal-regs.com) are superseded. Confidence: High.
- magnet-web's primitives are P-256 (ECDSA/ECDH), SHA-256, AES-GCM, HKDF, HMAC, Argon2id, BLAKE2b and PBKDF2, and the code goes on public repositories. v0.3 added constructions of its own over them (a Noise XX variant over P-256, a sealed box, record and media formats), which is the case this note said to re-check. See SECURITY.md for the decision and the classification note.
- Canada and EU dual-use rules were not verified this session; for a public repository with no sale the risk looks low, but confirm with counsel.

## What the docs must say (wording, ready to adapt)

- **PRIVACY.md, erasure:** "Deleting an entry removes its content from every copy that receives the deletion. It does not remove the entry's outline: who signed it (a public key) and when. Copies on devices that never sync again keep the content. For private records, deleting your key makes them unreadable to everyone, including you; copies of the scrambled data stay."
- **PRIVACY.md, chat:** "Chat messages are encrypted between identities. The site owner cannot read them and no one can scan them. Other participants and relays can see your IP address and when you are online."
- **PRIVACY.md, roles:** "The site owner decides why and how visitors' data is used and is responsible for it. Seeding is sharing: people who seed pass on what the site's ledger contains."
- **OPERATIONS.md:** "If you receive a legal notice, you can tombstone, ban and withdraw a version. You cannot make other people's copies disappear and you cannot read private chat. Say so in your reply."
- **ONBOARDING.md:** "Do not turn on chat, uploads or profiles for a site that children use unless you have an age-assurance method and have completed an assessment. This software cannot check anyone's age."
- **Not legal advice paragraph (all three docs):** "This describes how the software behaves and what published sources say. It is not legal advice. Laws differ by country and change. Ask a lawyer qualified where your visitors are before you collect personal data."
